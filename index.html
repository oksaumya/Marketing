1. Module 1 — Marketing Foundations

1.1 What is Marketing?

AMA Definition: Marketing is the process of creating, communicating, delivering, and exchanging offerings that have value for customers, clients, partners, and society at large.
Entrepreneurial Marketing: An innovative, proactive, risk-taking approach to marketing — focused on the unique value an entrepreneur delivers.

Marketing is interdisciplinary

  • Psychology — understanding consumer behavior (e.g. Amul's "Utterly Butterly Delicious" triggers nostalgia)
  • Economics — affordable luxury, economies of scale (Amul Butter pricing strategy)
  • Sociology — cultural integration ("Taste of India", Amul girl topical ads)
Case — Tata Nano Failure:
  • Psychology: Positioned as "cheap car" — clashed with aspirational consumers.
  • Sociology: Wrong target — middle-class wanted to "upgrade", not buy a downgrade-image car.
  • Economics: Cost vs perception mismatch — Maruti Alto offered better perceived value at a slightly higher price.

1.3 Why Marketing Matters in Entrepreneurship

  • Customer-Centric Approach — solving real pain points (Airbnb)
  • Brand Differentiation — Apple's "Think Different"
  • Scalability — small idea → global (Dropbox referral)
  • Feedback & Iteration — refine offerings continuously
Big Takeaway: "Entrepreneurship without marketing is innovation without impact." Marketing creates Awareness → Trust → Preference → Demand.

1.4 Components of Marketing

  • Market research — understand customers
  • STP — choose who to serve
  • 4 Ps / 7 Ps — execute strategy
  • Branding — build identity
  • Customer relationships — retain & grow
  • Analytics & measurement — what worked?

1.5 Types of Marketing

  • B2C — Business to Consumer (Amul → households)
  • B2B — Business to Business (Hyperpure → restaurants)
  • D2C — Direct to Consumer (Licious, Mamaearth)
  • Service Marketing — intangible offerings (banks, telecom)
  • Digital Marketing — SEO, SEM, social, influencer
  • Green / Cause Marketing — eco/ethical positioning
  • Guerrilla / Viral Marketing — unconventional, low-cost
  • Inbound vs Outbound — pull (content) vs push (cold outreach)

3. Marketing Environment & PESTEL Framework

Marketing Environment: Forces & factors affecting a company's ability to develop and maintain successful customer relationships.

Micro Factors (closer, somewhat controllable)

  • Suppliers
  • Customers
  • Competitors
  • Retailers/Intermediaries
  • Internal employees
Example: Walmart demands lower prices from Coca-Cola.

Macro Factors (broad, uncontrollable)

  • Economic conditions
  • Political / Legal
  • Social / Cultural
  • Technological
  • Environmental
Example: COVID-19 reshaped buying behavior globally.

The PESTEL Framework

LetterStands ForExamples
PPoliticalGovernment stability, tariffs, trade policy
EEconomicInflation, interest rates, GDP, exchange rates
SSocialDemographics, lifestyle, culture, education
TTechnological5G, UPI, automation, AI
EEnvironmentalClimate change, sustainability rules
LLegalConsumer protection, GST, data privacy
Memorise: Marketing success depends more on how well you adapt to the environment than how good your product is. Smart marketers don't fight the environment — they use it.

4.4 The 5-Step Consumer Decision-Making Process

Need Recognition → Information Search → Evaluation of Alternatives → Purchase Decision → Post-Purchase Behavior
  1. Need Recognition: Triggered by internal stimuli (hunger), external stimuli (ads), or situational (phone broke). Marketing doesn't create basic needs — it shapes wants and triggers recognition.
  2. Information Search: Personal (friends, family), commercial (ads, websites), public (reviews, forums).
  3. Evaluation of Alternatives: Comparing on price, quality, features. Marketers must highlight differentiators here.
  4. Purchase Decision: Influenced by promotions, store location, ease of purchase. Watch out for cart abandonment (unexpected costs, complicated checkout, security concerns).
  5. Post-Purchase Behavior: Satisfaction or dissonance → reviews, returns, repeat purchase, advocacy.
Cart abandonment recovery: Email recovery (~10–15%), retargeting ads (~25–30%). Use scarcity ("Only 5 left!"), free shipping thresholds, transparent pricing.

6. Market Research

Market Research: Process of gathering, analyzing, and interpreting information to understand consumer needs, market trends, and opportunities — following transparent, ethical, bias-free processes.

Why it Matters

  • Reduces risk before product launch
  • Identifies consumer preferences
  • Informs pricing, branding, advertising
  • Tests concepts & identifies market gaps
Before Maggi Oats Noodles, Nestlé ran taste tests to see if health-conscious Indians would adopt it.

6.1 Types of Research

PrimarySecondary
SourceFirsthand from consumersExisting reports, databases
MethodsSurveys, focus groups, interviews, observationGovt reports, industry papers, Google Trends
ExampleZomato surveyed 10K users before Zomato GoldSwiggy analyzed FSSAI reports before grocery entry
QualitativeQuantitative
PurposeUncover motivations, emotionsMeasure & validate
MethodsInterviews, focus groups, ethnographySurveys, polls, experiments
ExampleTanishq — emotional meaning of jewelleryUber — real-time surge pricing data

6.2 Research Techniques

  1. Surveys — quantitative, scale (Netflix preference surveys)
  2. Focus Groups — in-depth discussions (McDonald's → McSpicy from focus groups)
  3. Observational Research — watch real behavior (Starbucks adjusted seating after observation)
  4. Competitor Analysis — pricing, branding, reviews (PhonePe responded to Google Pay cashback)

6.6 Sampling Types

  • Random — every member has equal chance
  • Stratified — divide population into strata then sample
  • Convenience — easiest available (low quality)
  • Cluster — sample whole clusters/groups

6.4 The 5 Ps of the Research Process

  1. Purpose — why are we researching? (define problem)
  2. Plan — design methodology, sample, timeline
  3. Perform — execute data collection
  4. Process — analyse, interpret
  5. Present — translate insights into action for decision-makers

6.5 Survey Design Principles

  • Keep questions simple
  • Avoid leading questions
  • Maintain neutrality
  • Use scales (Likert, semantic differential)
  • Logical sequence

6.5 Challenges in Market Research

❌ Inadequate sample size
❌ Sampling bias (not representative)
❌ Questionnaire/interview bias (leading questions)
❌ Consumer bias — say they want X, but buy Y (Colgate herbal failed initially)
❌ Rapidly changing trends → outdated data

7. The 4 Ps (and 7 Ps) of Marketing Mix

7.1 Origin & Definition

Conceptualized in 1960 by E. Jerome McCarthy, influenced by Neil H. Borden's "marketing mix" (1950s).

"Developing the right product and making it available at the right place with the right promotion at the right price to satisfy target consumers and meet business objectives."

7.2 The Classic 4 Ps

PQuestion AnsweredTagline
ProductWhat is offered?Make what people need and desire
PriceWhat do customers pay?Set it at what they're willing to pay
PlaceWhere is it available?Sell it where they shop
PromotionHow is it communicated?Tell them why they need it NOW

7.3 Characteristics of Services (4 I's)

CharacteristicMeaningMarketing Challenge
IntangibilityCannot be touched, seen, tasted before purchaseUse "physical evidence" to make tangible
InseparabilityProduced & consumed simultaneously (haircut)Quality depends on the service person
PerishabilityCannot be stored (empty flight seat)Demand management via dynamic pricing
VariabilityQuality varies by who/when/whereStandardize via training & process

7.4 The Extended 7 Ps (for Services)

Because of the 4 I's, services need 3 additional Ps:

  • People — staff training, customer interaction (hotel front desk)
  • Process — service delivery efficiency (Zomato app flow)
  • Physical Evidence — tangibles (uniforms, ambience, packaging, branded receipts)
Important: STP defines strategy. 4 Ps execute strategy. If STP is wrong, no amount of 4P tweaking can save the product.

8. Product Strategy

8.1 What is a Product?

Not just a physical item — a bundle of benefits that satisfies customer needs.

Customers don't buy things — they buy solutions, experiences, and value.

8.2 Product Levels / Layers

LayerMeaningSmartphone Example
Core ProductFundamental benefitCommunication, connectivity
Actual ProductTangible features (easiest to copy)Phone, camera, OS, design
Augmented ProductExtra value (where competition is won)Warranty, service, apps, EMI
Core needs change slowly. Actual products are easy to imitate. Augmented benefits create differentiation — that's why Amazon is hard to beat even at similar prices.

8.3 Product Life Cycle (PLC)

GrowthRising fastIncreasingDifferentiate, expand distributionPersuade

8.4 Rogers' 5 Adopter Categories (1962)

  1. Innovators (2.5%) — risk-takers, tech enthusiasts
  2. Early Adopters (13.5%) — opinion leaders
  3. Early Majority (34%) — deliberate, pragmatic
  4. Late Majority (34%) — skeptical, follow majority
  5. Laggards (16%) — tradition-bound

8.5 Crossing the Chasm (Geoffrey Moore)

The hardest gap is between Early Adopters and the Early Majority — the visionaries are saturated but the mainstream isn't ready.

Bowling Pin Strategy: Win one niche completely → use it as entry point → knock down adjacent segments. Allocate resources, form partnerships, build the whole product.

8.6 New Product Development (NPD)

NPD is about survival, not creativity. Most new products fail.

Common reasons products fail

  • No real customer need
  • Poor positioning
  • Wrong pricing
  • Weak promotion
  • Managerial overconfidence
Famous Failures: Google Glass, Pepsi Blue, Tata Nano

9. Pricing Strategy

Critical: Pricing is the only P that generates revenue. All others are costs. Customers use price as a proxy for quality.

9.1 Pricing Objectives

ObjectiveWhen UsedExample
SurvivalCrisis, excess capacity, intense competitionAirlines discounting in COVID
Profit MaximizationLimited competition, strong differentiationLuxury perfumes
Market Share GrowthMass market, economies of scaleReliance Jio launch
Premium / PrestigeStatus & exclusivityRolex (price IS the value)

9.2 Pricing Approaches

Cost-Based Pricing

Price = Cost + Markup — simple but ignores willingness to pay. Best for government contracts, B2B manufacturing.

Limitations: Customers don't care about your costs. May underprice valuable products or overprice weak ones.

Value-Based Pricing

Price based on perceived customer value, not cost. Requires deep customer understanding.

Starbucks charges premium for experience, not coffee. Movie popcorn (cost ₹20–30, price ₹250–400). Software pricing based on productivity gains. Brand equity supports premium pricing.

9.3 Launch Pricing Strategies

Penetration Pricing 📉

Low initial price → rapid adoption → deter competitors.

Used when: Market is price-sensitive, scale economies exist.
Risk: Customers anchor low expectations.
Example: Netflix introductory pricing.

Skimming Pricing 📈

High initial price → target early adopters → lower price over time.

Used when: Innovative products, tech markets, recover R&D fast.
Example: iPhone launches.

9.4 Other Pricing Concepts

  • Psychological pricing — ₹999 instead of ₹1000
  • Bundle pricing — combo meals, software suites
  • Dynamic pricing — Uber surge, airline tickets
  • Freemium — free base + paid premium (Spotify)
Pricing must align with: STP decisions, brand positioning, competitive environment. Wrong pricing kills even great products. Always ask: What problem is the price solving?

10.1 Why Distribution Matters

Distribution creates 3 utilities: Time, Place, Possession. A great product fails if customers can't easily access it.

10.6 Omnichannel Distribution

Seamless integration of online + offline → buy online, pickup in-store; browse in-store, order online.

Challenges: Inventory sync, channel conflict (Nike online cheaper than store), tech integration, consistent service.

10.7 Channel Conflict

  • Vertical — between channel levels (manufacturer vs wholesaler over margins)
  • Horizontal — between same-level players (two wholesalers in same territory)
  • Multichannel — online undercuts offline
Strategic insight: Your channel dependency can become your biggest risk. If Swiggy shuts down tomorrow, which brands suffer first?

12. Marketing Frameworks — SWOT, BCG, Ansoff, AIDA

12.1 SWOT Analysis — The Diagnostic Lens

Internal (Inside firm)External (Market/world)
HelpfulStrengthsOpportunities
HarmfulWeaknessesThreats
Zomato SWOT
S: Massive user data (knows what India eats at 11 PM)
W: Dependence on human delivery partners
O: Quick commerce boom (Blinkit acquisition)
T: D2C kitchen apps bypassing aggregators
SWOT is NOT strategy — it's a decision lens. It tells you what to think about, not what to do. Strategic Matching: use Strengths to grab Opportunities. Defensive Positioning: identify which Threats exploit your Weaknesses.

12.2 BCG Matrix — Resource Allocation

Two axes: Market Growth (high/low) × Relative Market Share (high/low)

QuadrantGrowthShareStrategyExample
⭐ StarsHighHighFund aggressivelyJio 4G launch, Zepto today
🐄 Cash CowsLowHighMaintain & optimize — milk for fundsHDFC core savings, Zerodha brokerage
❓ Question MarksHighLowExperiment selectively, kill fast if data badTata Motors EV, BluSmart
🐶 DogsLowLowKill / divestBSNL landline, TinyOwl
Use of cash flow: Cash Cows fund Stars and selected Question Marks. Dogs are killed to free up resources.

12.3 Ansoff Matrix — Growth Strategy

2×2: Products (existing/new) × Markets (existing/new). Risk increases left → right and down.

Existing ProductNew Product
Existing Market Market Penetration (lowest risk) — sell more to current customers (more outlets, lower price, more ads) Product Development — new products to existing customers (Amul protein drinks)
New Market Market Development — enter new segments/regions (Tier-3 expansion, foreign markets) Diversification (highest risk) — Related (Apple Watch) or Unrelated (Tata Tea + Trucks)
Related Diversification — synergies with existing business (Apple Watch leverages iOS ecosystem).
Unrelated Diversification — no synergies (Tata Tea has no impact on Tata Trucks if a batch fails).

12.4 AIDA Model — The Conversion Funnel

Attention → Interest → Desire → Action
  • Attention 👀 — break through the noise (eye-catching ad)
  • Interest 🤔 — explain the "how" and "why"
  • Desire ❤️ — move from "that's cool" to "I need that"
  • Action ✅ — final click, purchase, sign-up

Extensions of AIDA

  • AIDCAS — Attention, Interest, Desire, Confidence, Action, Satisfaction
  • REAN — Reach, Engage, Activate, Nurture
  • NAITDASE — Need, Attention, Interest, Trust, Design, Action, Satisfaction, Evaluation
Linking frameworks: SWOT (where are we) → PESTEL (environment) → Ansoff (where to grow) → AIDA (how to convert).

12.5 Porter's 5 Forces — Industry Attractiveness

Analyse competitive intensity & profitability potential of an industry.

ForceQuestionHigh Force = Bad For You When...
Rivalry among existing competitorsHow intense is the fight?Many similar-sized players, slow growth, high exit barriers
Threat of new entrantsHow easy is it to enter?Low capital needs, no patents, easy distribution
Bargaining power of suppliersCan suppliers dictate terms?Few suppliers, no substitutes for their input
Bargaining power of buyersCan customers force prices down?Few large buyers, undifferentiated product
Threat of substitutesCan buyers switch to alternatives?Many substitutes, low switching cost
Indian airline industry: high rivalry (Indigo, Air India, SpiceJet), high buyer power (price-sensitive), low entry barriers thanks to leasing → low profitability.

13. Sales Management & Distribution Channels

13.1 Sales vs Marketing

MarketingSales
Creates the pull through awarenessCreates the push to close the transaction
Long-term demand frameworkShort-term transaction closure
Brand, segments, positioningFunnel, quotas, conversions
High-performing firms have Sales + Marketing aligned, not siloed.

13.2 The Sales Funnel & Leakage

Awareness → Interest → Consideration → Intent → Evaluation → Purchase → Retention/Advocacy

Funnel Leakage: Customers drop off at each stage. Diagnose where leakage is highest:

  • Top leak → weak awareness/advertising
  • Middle leak → poor positioning, weak differentiation
  • Bottom leak → bad sales process, pricing, friction at checkout

13.3 Sales Metrics vs Marketing Metrics

Sales MetricsMarketing Metrics
Revenue, units sold, conversion rateReach, impressions, brand awareness, share of voice
Sales cycle length, win rateCAC (Customer Acquisition Cost)
Quota attainment, AOVNPS, brand recall, CTR, engagement
Short-term, transactionalLong-term, perception-driven

13.4 Sales Forecasting Basics

  • Historical trends — past sales extrapolated
  • Market potential method — TAM × penetration
  • Salesforce estimates — rep-level pipeline
  • Buyer intention surveys — ask customers directly
  • Statistical / AI models — regression, time-series

13.5 Types of Sales

  • Inside Sales — remote (calls, emails, Zoom). E.g. Bengaluru SaaS firms selling globally.
  • Field Sales / Personal Selling — face-to-face, high-touch (B2B, pharma reps)
  • Consultative Selling — diagnose customer needs, then prescribe (insurance done right)

13.6 Sales Organisation Structures

  • Geographic — by territory (most common). Pros: low travel cost, local knowledge. Cons: rep handles all products.
  • Product-Based — reps specialize in product lines. Pros: deep expertise. Cons: overlap if multiple reps visit same client.
  • Customer-Based — reps focus on customer types/key accounts (KAM). Pros: customer-centric. Cons: requires senior reps.
  • Hybrid — combination, used by large MNCs.

13.7 Territory Design & Quotas

Balance workload and sales potential across regions. Set quotas based on potential, history, difficulty. Quotas drive motivation but bad quotas drive unethical selling.

13.8 Sales Training, Incentives & Analytics

  • Training: product knowledge, soft skills, objection handling, CRM tools
  • Incentives: commissions, bonuses, contests, recognition, non-monetary perks
  • Analytics: pipeline health, win/loss analysis, churn prediction, rep productivity

13.9 Channel Power Sources (French & Raven)

13.10 Strategic Alliances & Negotiation

Toyota–Suzuki India alliance (shared platforms: Grand Vitara, Hyryder) — pooled resources, faster market entry.
  • Platform Partnerships — e.g. brand on Amazon marketplace
  • Co-branding — Intel Inside on laptops
  • Distribution alliances — sharing logistics
Alliance risks: loss of control, conflicting objectives, IP leakage, dependency.

13.11 Trade Promotion Strategies (push tactics)

  • Volume discounts to wholesalers/retailers
  • Slotting fees / shelf placement support
  • Co-op advertising funds
  • Sales contests for channel partners
  • Free goods with bulk orders

13.12 Ethics in Selling

Unethical: An insurance agent selling an unsuitable policy just to meet quota → violates ethics in selling. Always: customer interest > quota.

13.8 D2C (Direct-to-Consumer) Advantages

  • Full control over customer experience
  • Access to first-party data
  • Higher margins (no intermediaries)
  • Build direct brand relationships
Licious — D2C meat brand uses this for premium quality control and customer data.

14. Advertising & Media Planning

14.1 Why Advertising Matters

  • Creates awareness before sales interaction
  • Builds legitimacy for new brands
  • Reinforces positioning over time
  • Shapes memory before need arises
You may not need insurance today — but repeated LIC ads ensure recall when you do.

14.3 Advertising Objectives (classical)

  • Inform — create knowledge (new products)
  • Persuade — shape preference (competitive markets)
  • Remind — reinforce memory (mature brands)

14.4 Media Planning — Reach × Frequency × Impact

DimensionMeaningWhen It Matters Most
Reach% of unique target audience exposed at least onceNew launches, awareness goals
FrequencyAvg. number of times each person sees adCompetitive categories, complex messages
ImpactQuality/memorability of each exposure (IPL ad > random YouTube ad)Premium brands, emotional appeals

GRP (Gross Rating Points) = Reach × Frequency — the standard media buying currency.

Budget trade-off: Reach 5M once vs Reach 1M five times. New launch → reach. Established product → frequency. For premium brands, 1 high-impact exposure often beats 5 low-impact ones; for FMCG, frequency wins.

14.5 Traditional vs Digital Media

DimensionTraditionalDigital
ReachBroadTargeted
MeasurementLimitedHighly measurable
SpeedSlowerImmediate
FlexibilityLowerHigh
Premium luxury watch launch in India → best media: Airport outdoor + luxury magazines, not mass YouTube ads (which would dilute brand).

14.6 When Media Planning Goes Wrong

Luxury brand advertising heavily on discount platforms / low-quality YouTube → high reach but low conversion + damaged brand perception. Media must align with positioning.

15. IMC — Integrated Marketing Communication

IMC: Coordinating all communication tools so the brand speaks with one clear voice. Core principle: Consistency creates credibility.

15.3 Common IMC Mistakes

❌ Too many agencies → different tones
❌ Weak coordination between marketing & sales
❌ Promotions launched without channel readiness
❌ Inconsistent visuals
❌ Strategic / operational / channel misalignment
Bad IMC Customer Journey: Instagram ad = premium → Website = confusing → Store = discount-heavy. Customer feels Confused, not Loyal.
Strong IMC examples: Apple ecosystem (premium across all touchpoints), Nykaa (consistent beauty narrative across app/website/store).

16. Multi-Level Marketing (MLM)

MLM: A distribution model built on networks, incentives, and personal selling. Distributors earn from their sales and from sales of those they recruit (downlines).

16.1 Why Firms Use MLM

  • Lower retail infrastructure cost
  • Rapid network expansion
  • Direct personal persuasion
  • Strong word-of-mouth leverage

16.2 Common MLM Product Categories

  • Health & nutrition supplements
  • Personal care / cosmetics
  • Wellness products
  • Household items

16.3 MLM vs Pyramid Scheme

MLM (Legal)Pyramid (Illegal)
Genuine products are soldNo real product / token product
Commissions tied to salesCommissions tied to recruitment
Recruitment supplements salesRecruitment dominates compensation

16.4 Pros & Cons

✅ Advantages

  • Low entry barriers
  • Entrepreneurial opportunity
  • Flexible schedule
  • Relationship-based selling

❌ Criticisms

  • Earnings concentrated at top
  • Recruitment pressure
  • Difficult long-term sustainability
  • High dropout rates

16.5 Famous MLM Examples

  • Amway — household + wellness, distributor-led relationship selling
  • Herbalife — nutrition-community model; subject of major FTC settlement
India: Direct Selling Rules (2016/Consumer Protection Rules 2021) regulate MLMs — buy-back guarantee, no money circulation, recruitment ≠ pyramid.

17. Loyalty, Retention & Relationship Marketing

Critical stats: Retaining customers is 5× cheaper than acquiring new ones. A 5% improvement in retention can boost profits by 25–95%.

17.1 What is a Loyalty Program?

A reward-based strategy to encourage repeat purchases through incentives, discounts, points, or exclusive perks.

Why Loyalty Matters

  • Reduces customer churn
  • Encourages repeat purchases
  • Builds brand advocacy
  • Cheaper than acquisition

17.2 Drivers of TRUE Loyalty

  • Rewards — points, cashback
  • Habit — buying without thinking
  • Switching cost — hard to leave
  • Brand trust — emotional connection (the strongest driver)

17.3 Types of Loyalty Programs

TypeHow It WorksExample
Points-BasedEarn points per purchase, redeem for discountsShoppers Stop First Citizen, Starbucks Rewards
Tiered MembershipHigher spend → higher tier & benefitsTaj InnerCircle (Gold/Silver/Platinum), Airlines

CoalitionPoints across multiple brandsReliance HappyPoints (Reliance Fresh, Digital, Trends)
GamifiedChallenges, milestones, quizzesZomato Gold, Myntra Insiders

17.4 How to Build a Successful Loyalty Program (6 Steps)

  1. Define objectives & target audience (repeat purchases? referrals? engagement?)
  2. Choose program type (points / tier / paid / cashback / gamified)
  3. Structure the rewards (earning rules, redemption options, perceived value)
  4. Digital & omnichannel integration (app tracking, AI-personalisation)
  5. Promote the program (ads, referrals, influencers)
  6. Track, analyze, improve (engagement, redemption rates, feedback)

17.5 Best Practices

  • Personalization — Amazon's recommendations based on purchase history
  • Easy enrollment — Nike Membership simple sign-up
  • Attractive, achievable rewards — Ulta Rewards (quick redemption keeps customers shopping)
  • Omnichannel access — Domino's "Wow Wednesday" app-exclusive deals

17.6 Key Loyalty Metrics & Formulas

17.7 The Dark Side of Loyalty Programs

Programs fail when: rewards aren't valuable enough, redemption is too hard, members feel surveilled, or competitors offer easier alternatives. Over-discounting in loyalty = brand dilution.

17.8 Future of Loyalty

  • AI & Personalisation — highly tailored rewards
  • Gamification — challenges, achievements
  • Sustainability & CSR — reward eco-friendly actions
  • Blockchain — secure, transparent reward systems

17B. Brand Equity, Branding & CRM (Quick Reference)

Brand Equity — Why brands command premium

Brand Equity = the value a brand adds to a product, over and above its functional benefits. It's why Apple commands premium over identical-spec phones.

Keller's CBBE Pyramid (bottom → top)

  1. Salience — Who are you? (Brand awareness)
  2. Performance & Imagery — What are you? (Functional + symbolic)
  3. Judgments & Feelings — What do I think/feel about you?
  4. Resonance — What about you and me? (Deep loyalty, advocacy)

Customer Relationship Management (CRM)

  • Data & Interactions — collect customer data across touchpoints
  • Personalising offerings — segment-specific deals, recommendations
  • Retaining profitable customers — focus on high-CLV segments
Amazon Recommendations use purchase history + browsing data to deliver personalised offers — classic CRM in action.

Relationship Marketing

Long-term customer relationship building — moves beyond transactions to partnership.

  • Repeat business through trust
  • Two-way communication (feedback loops)
  • Lifetime value focus over single-sale focus
  • Personalisation at scale via AI/ML

18. Final Integration — The Big Picture

18.1 Marketing is NOT just 4 Ps

It is an integrated system connecting strategy, execution, scale, and ethics:

Module 1 (Foundations: WHO to target) → Module 2 (Strategy Engine: HOW to win) → Module 3 (Execution: HOW to execute) → Module 4 (Digital: HOW to scale & sustain)

18.2 The Integrated Marketing System

  1. Foundations — STP, Consumer Behavior, Environment
  2. Strategy Engine — Frameworks (SWOT/BCG/Ansoff), 4Ps, Branding
  3. Execution — Sales, Distribution, Advertising, IMC
  4. Sustain — Loyalty/CRM, Digital, Analytics, Ethics

18.3 Common Marketing Mistakes

❌ Ignoring STP — building before knowing who for
❌ Over-emphasizing one P (great product, weak distribution)
❌ Inconsistent execution (premium ads, mass discounting)
❌ Short-term focus / discount addiction
❌ Poor coordination across teams
❌ Lack of customer insight

18.4 Ethics & Sustainability in Marketing

Modern marketing must balance profit, planet, and people (triple bottom line).

  • Green marketing — promote eco-friendly products (Tata's EV push)
  • Ethical advertising — no false claims, no harmful stereotypes
  • Fair pricing — no exploitation of vulnerable consumers
  • Data privacy — GDPR, India DPDP Act 2023
  • Honest selling — no misleading customers (insurance mis-selling = ethics violation)
  • Sustainability reporting — ESG metrics
Examples of unethical marketing: hidden fees, dark patterns, fake reviews, exaggerated MLM income claims, anti-aging cream over-promises.

18.5 Key Trade-offs Every Marketer Faces

  • Reach vs Frequency — wide vs deep
  • Brand vs Performance — long-term equity vs short-term sales
  • Penetration vs Skimming — share vs margins
  • Standardization vs Localization
  • Direct vs Indirect channels
  • Acquisition vs Retention

18.6 The Manager Mindset

Think in trade-offs, not absolutes. Think in customer journeys, not single touchpoints. Think in integrated systems, not isolated Ps. Diagnose problems before prescribing solutions (SWOT → root cause → fix).

18.7 Final Takeaway

🎯 Remember Above All

  • Marketing creates Awareness → Trust → Preference → Demand
  • STP defines, 4Ps execute — get STP wrong and no P can save you
  • The only revenue-generating P is Price; all others are costs
  • Augmented layer is where competition is won, not Core or Actual
  • Retention is 5× cheaper than acquisition
  • Consistency builds credibility — IMC is the glue
  • Smart marketers don't fight the environment — they use it
