1 2 Marks Travel company — same ad & tagline across Insta, YouTube, TV, hoardings Identify the marketing communication concept.

Concept: Integrated Marketing Communications (IMC).

Justification: IMC coordinates all promotional tools so the brand speaks with one consistent voice. Using the same tagline and visuals across Instagram, YouTube, TV and OOH ensures unified brand identity, increases recall, builds credibility, and avoids customer confusion. Consistency is the core principle of IMC.

2 2 Marks Mobile brand — low introductory price to capture share Identify pricing strategy and state one risk.

Strategy: Penetration Pricing.

Risk: Customers may anchor on the low price as the "true value" of the product. When the company later tries to raise prices, customers may resist or switch — this is called price anchoring and it also risks brand dilution, making the brand seem cheap or low-quality.

3 2 Marks Car company conducts focus group on SUV vs sedan preference Identify the type of marketing research.

Type: Qualitative Primary Research (specifically, a Focus Group Discussion).

Explanation: Focus groups are firsthand (primary) research that uses small group discussions to uncover motivations, emotions, beliefs — qualitative insights. They help marketers understand the "why" behind customer preferences, which structured surveys often cannot capture.

4 2 Marks Water purifier — "safest choice for your family" Which positioning strategy?

Strategy: Benefit-Based Positioning (with strong emotional appeal).

Justification: The brand emphasises a specific benefit — safety for the family — that resonates emotionally with the target buyer (typically parents). This positioning creates clear differentiation and an emotional reason to prefer the brand over competitors who may compete on price or features.

5 2 Marks Electronics brand portfolio analysis Which framework is best suited?

Framework: BCG Matrix (Boston Consulting Group).

Justification: BCG plots products on Market Growth Rate × Relative Market Share, classifying them as Stars (high/high), Cash Cows (low/high), Question Marks (high/low) and Dogs (low/low). This is ideal for portfolio decisions — what to fund, milk, experiment with, or kill. Perfectly suited to evaluating the brand's 5 products.

PART – B  |  Answer all Questions (4 × 3 = 12 Marks)
6 3 Marks EV startup launching electric scooters in India — PESTEL Explain any three macro factors.
  1. Political: Strong government push for EVs via FAME-II subsidies, state-level EV policies (Delhi, Maharashtra). Favourable for the startup but policy changes can disrupt cost structures.
  2. Economic: Rising petrol/diesel prices, falling battery costs, and improving disposable income make EVs more attractive. Inflation in lithium & copper could however raise costs.
  3. Technological: Rapid advances in battery tech, charging infrastructure, swappable batteries enable better range & user convenience. Startups must keep up to remain competitive.

(Other relevant: Social — climate consciousness among Gen Z; Environmental — pollution control; Legal — vehicle safety norms, FAME registration.)

7 3 Marks Penetration vs Skimming pricing (a) Define both. (b) Recommend for budget college students.

(a) Definitions:

  • Penetration Pricing: Setting a low initial price to attract maximum customers quickly, build market share, and deter competitors. Suitable for price-sensitive mass markets with scale economies. Example: Reliance Jio.
  • Skimming Pricing: Setting a high initial price to extract maximum margin from early adopters; gradually reducing it to attract more segments. Suitable for innovative tech products. Example: iPhone launches.

(b) Best Fit for Budget College Students: Penetration Pricing.

Justification: College students are highly price-sensitive with limited disposable income. A low launch price drives rapid adoption, builds early brand loyalty, and benefits from word-of-mouth in a tightly-networked segment. Scale economies later allow profitability.

10 9 Marks Case Study — Reliance Jio's Disruptive Entry
Context Recap: In 2016 Jio offered free 4G data + voice calls for 6 months — acquired 100M+ subscribers in < 6 months. Today Jio is the telecom leader and has expanded into broadband, JioMart, JioCinema.

(a) Pricing Strategy + STP Approach at Launch (4 Marks):

Pricing Strategy: Penetration Pricing (Free → Low). Jio gave away service free of cost to disrupt the price anchor of competitors. Once locked in, gradual paid plans were introduced. Goal: maximum customer acquisition + market share before competitors could react.

STP:

  • Segmentation: Geographic (pan-India), Demographic (all income groups), Behavioural (data consumers).
  • Targeting: Mass-market, especially price-sensitive consumers who were paying high prices to Airtel/Vodafone for limited data. Also targeted first-time data users.
  • Positioning: "Digital India for everyone — high-speed internet at the lowest cost." Backed by Mukesh Ambani's vision & the Reliance brand.

(b) Ansoff Matrix for Jio's Growth Beyond Telecom (5 Marks):

QuadrantJio's MoveStrategy

11 9 Marks Case Study — Maggi's 2015 Comeback
Context Recap: Maggi was banned in 2015 over alleged lead/MSG content. Nestlé recalled stock, re-cleared safety tests, re-launched with new packaging, emotional storytelling ("We missed you too"), social media push and kirana re-engagement. Regained market leadership within a year.

(a) Two Promotional Strategies + Importance (4 Marks):

  1. Emotional Storytelling Campaign ("We Missed You Too"): Nestlé used a heart-warming tone acknowledging Maggi's absence from Indian kitchens. This rebuilt the emotional bond with consumers, leveraged nostalgia, and transformed the crisis narrative into a comeback story. Emotional appeals work powerfully for legacy brands.
  2. Heavy Social Media + Influencer Engagement: Hashtags like #WeMissYouToo went viral; user-generated content flooded Instagram/Twitter. This amplified reach at low cost, made the comeback feel community-driven and authentic, and rapidly rebuilt trust.

(Alternative: Sales promotion — discounted re-launch packs; Kirana POS displays.)

(b) Role of IMC in Rebuilding Trust (5 Marks):

  1. Consistent Message Across Channels: Whether the audience saw a TV ad, a tweet, a kirana shelf or a packaging insert — the message was unified: safe, beloved, back. This consistency rebuilt credibility, which was the central need after a safety scare.
  2. Multiple Touchpoints Reinforced Each Other: TV created scale awareness; social media drove conversation; PR (third-party safety certifications) provided credibility; retail re-stocking ensured availability — all coordinated.
  3. Sales + Marketing Alignment: Re-engaging with kirana retailers ensured product was available the moment ads aired — preventing customer disappointment and channel breakage.
  4. Crisis Recovery Required Coherence: Inconsistent messaging during a crisis could have re-ignited doubt. IMC's "one voice" principle was essential.
  5. Trust Compounding: Repeated consistent exposure across channels created the perception that "Maggi has truly cleared the issue", enabling rapid share recovery within 12 months.
Conclusion: IMC transformed a near-fatal brand crisis into a successful comeback — proving that consistency builds credibility, especially when trust is the central currency.
12 9 Marks Case Study — Tata Nano: The Failed Dream
Context Recap: Tata launched Nano in 2008 as the world's cheapest car at ₹1 lakh. Despite heavy advertising, customers perceived it as a "cheap car", lacking status & safety. Sales never picked up; discontinued in 2018.

(a) Marketing Mix & STP Mistakes (4 Marks):

  1. Product: Utilitarian, basic design — failed to evoke pride or excitement. Lacked features and safety perception.
  2. Price: The "₹1 lakh = cheapest car in the world" message backfired. Price became a stigma, not an attraction. Indian car-buyers see cars as status symbols, not just transport.
  3. Promotion: Advertising emphasised cost over aspiration, reinforcing the negative association of "cheap".
  4. STP Mistake — Wrong Target: Tata targeted two-wheeler upgraders, but this segment was aspirational — they wanted to be seen as upgrading socially. The Nano made them feel they had bought down, not up.
  5. Wrong Positioning: Positioned as the "people's car" / "cheapest car" → eroded social value. Aspirational segments avoided it; existing car owners saw no reason to switch.

(b) Positioning, Consumer Perception & Re-Positioning (5 Marks):

Importance of Positioning & Consumer Perception:

  • Positioning is the image of the brand in the customer's mind — it determines purchase intent more than actual features.
  • Consumer perception is shaped by emotional cues — pride, status, fear, aspiration. A car priced too low signals low quality, low safety, low status.
  • In India, vehicles are identity markers. People upgrade for visible social progression, not just practicality.
  • A great product can fail if customers perceive it negatively. Perception > Product.

How Tata Could Have Re-Positioned the Nano:

  1. "The Smart City Car": Emphasise innovation, fuel efficiency, urban convenience, parking ease — not lowest price.
  2. Target the "Second Car" Segment: Reposition for middle-class families needing a second car for the wife/children/short trips. This avoids the "buying down" stigma.
  3. Lifestyle Positioning: Use vibrant colours, youth ambassadors, fun urban-life branding. Show stylish young couples / college students.
  4. Re-Price Upward: Counter-intuitive but powerful — pricing at say ₹2.5–3 lakh with better features removes the "cheap car" stigma.
  5. Engineer Premium Variants: Add safety features (airbags, ABS), modern infotainment, premium interiors — to break the perception ceiling.
Conclusion: Tata Nano is a classic case of how a strong product engineering achievement was destroyed by wrong positioning. What's in the consumer's mind matters more than what's under the hood.
